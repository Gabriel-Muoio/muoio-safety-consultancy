Being a safety professional can sometimes feel like belonging between places.

You work alongside management, but you depend on the trust and respect of workers. You are expected to uphold standards, investigate problems and advise the organisation, yet much of the information you need is held by people who may regard you as part of the authority structure.

Nobody wants to be injured at work. But accepting a familiar physical risk can feel easier than accepting the social risk of being the person who stops the job.

You can see this tension during a toolbox meeting.

A supervisor asks whether anybody has questions, concerns or complaints. More often than not, the response is silence. People look at the ground, shuffle their paperwork or examine a coffee cup as though the answer might be written on it.

Stick around after the management representatives have left, however, and the conversation may begin.

A piece of equipment has been broken for weeks. A procedure doesn’t make sense. Someone’s behaviour is making other people dread coming to work. A task cannot be completed using the method everyone has just agreed to follow.

When workers bring these things to you, particularly when nobody else is listening, it means you may have demonstrated something that is difficult to gain and remarkably easy to lose: reasonableness.

## Fear produces clean answers

Does a worker become uncomfortable when you approach? Do they straighten up, become guarded and hope they don’t do anything that attracts your attention or gives off “unsafe vibes”?

That isn’t necessarily respect. They may simply fear you.

Fear can create the appearance of control. Workers say the job is safe. Pre-starts are completed without disagreement. Procedures are signed. Nobody raises an awkward question in front of the group.

The safety professional receives a succession of reassuring answers and may conclude that the workforce is engaged. In reality, the conversation may have been shaped to exclude precisely the information the organisation needs.

Consider a worker who tells you that, for the past three weeks, purlins have been rested on the handrail of a scissor lift while riggers quickly pin them into position.

How do you respond?

Do you wait for the blood to return to your face before sending the worker to the site office for an incident interview? Do you tell them to keep it quiet so the project can reach the end of the month without another event to report? Or do you hear them out and ask why the work has been done that way?

Your response will teach more than the next toolbox talk ever could.

The worker has not only disclosed an unsafe practice. They have tested what happens when reality is brought into the room. Other workers will learn from the result, whether they witness the conversation or hear about it afterwards.

If disclosure leads immediately to humiliation or punishment, the organisation may not eliminate the practice. It may simply eliminate the reporting of it. That is a dangerous trade: the work remains unchanged, but management becomes less able to see it.

## Reasonableness is not inconsistency

Workers may accuse a safety professional of being inconsistent when one person receives coaching while another matter is investigated formally. The criticism deserves attention. Arbitrary decisions destroy trust.

But consistency does not require identical outcomes. It requires the same principles to be applied fairly to the relevant facts.

Two incidents may appear similar while differing in important ways: the worker’s knowledge and intent, the availability of a safe alternative, the adequacy of planning and supervision, the seriousness of the risk, the organisation’s previous tolerance of the practice and the worker’s willingness to be honest about what occurred.

Treating those cases identically would not necessarily be consistent. It may simply ignore the circumstances.

To be reasonable is to consider those circumstances before deciding what the situation means. It is to examine the conditions surrounding an action rather than concentrating only on the final piece in the sequence—often a worker making a poor decision.

A reasonable safety professional can prioritise useful information over the immediate satisfaction of disciplining somebody. They can defer judgement until the important pieces are in place. They can consult workers with a genuine interest in learning and admit when their first interpretation was wrong.

This doesn’t require abandoning standards or avoiding difficult decisions. It means being consistent in method without pretending that every case is the same.

Sometimes the appropriate response will still be firm. Trust doesn’t require pretending that reckless behaviour is acceptable. Workers are more likely to accept an unwelcome decision, however, when they believe the person making it has listened carefully, separated fact from noise and considered the matter fairly.

## Learning not to decide too early

When I first began working as a safety professional, I could be thrown around by workers’ subjective accounts of a situation or incident.

Someone would give a convincing explanation and I would feel certain that I knew who—or what—was responsible. Then I would interview somebody else and the picture would appear to flip. More investigation would reveal another condition, another decision or another explanation. By the end of the day, the position that had seemed obvious that morning might no longer be defensible.

The lesson was not that workers were unreliable. Each person was describing the event from a position shaped by what they had seen, what they knew and what the outcome meant for them.

A supervisor might understand the planning decisions but not the difficulty of performing the task. A worker might understand the task intimately but know little about the constraints imposed on the supervisor. A manager might see a departure from the system without knowing how routinely that departure had been tolerated.

Each account could be sincere without being complete.

I learned not to make early judgements about a person’s attitude, opinion or version of events. I also stopped promising workers that I would do this or that in response to what they told me. Instead, I made it clear that I was listening and gathering information for later use.

Listening is not agreement. It is not an undertaking to endorse the speaker’s interpretation or deliver the outcome they want—it is a commitment to understand what they know before deciding what should happen.

Workers may speak dismissively about a colleague, complain about their rate of pay or express themselves with colourful language and an excited temperament. Not everything said in the course of a disclosure is relevant, and it is not always the safety professional’s business to correct the speaker’s tone.

The words begin falling through a lattice, so to speak, separating useful information from noise.

What happened? What conditions were present? What had become normal? What did the person believe was expected of them? What changed? What prevented the safer option from being used?

The corrective actions that follow will not always please the person who raised the issue. They will not necessarily match that person’s interpretation of events. But they should be reasonably practicable and address what sits at the heart of the problem—not penalise an individual for being visible at the end of it.

## Better questions require a better listener

Workers often answer “yes” when asked whether a job is safe because the question already contains the expected answer.

“Is everyone happy with the job?”

“Do we all understand the procedure?”

“Is it safe to start?”

Asked publicly by someone with authority, these questions invite agreement. A worker who says no must interrupt the meeting, challenge the plan and accept some responsibility for whatever delay follows.

Silence in that setting doesn’t necessarily indicate consent. It may indicate that the cost of disagreeing has not been made safe enough.

Changing the words can help. A supervisor might ask:

-   What is most likely to make this job depart from the plan?
-   What part of the task is harder in practice than it appears on paper?
-   What will people be tempted to improvise if the correct equipment is unavailable?
-   Where do we expect the greatest pressure to keep going?
-   What would make you stop the job?
-   What have we missed?

These questions do not ask workers to declare the job unsafe in front of their peers. They assume that complications are possible and give the group something concrete to examine.

Even then, the quality of the answers will depend on what workers expect to happen next.

No question, however carefully phrased, can compensate for an unreasonable response. If a supervisor asks what has been missed and then becomes defensive when somebody answers, the workforce will remember the response more clearly than the invitation.

If a safety professional asks for honesty and treats every disclosure as a confession, workers will learn to provide safer answers rather than better information.

## Confidentiality has limits, but discretion matters

Workers may choose to speak after a meeting because they don’t want to raise an issue publicly. That preference should not be mistaken for cowardice or disloyalty. They may be trying to protect working relationships, avoid embarrassing a supervisor or prevent themselves from being labelled as difficult.

A safety professional cannot promise that everything disclosed will remain confidential. Serious risks, allegations of harmful conduct and matters requiring investigation may need to be escalated.

But there is a difference between confidentiality and discretion.

Discretion means not exposing the source of information unnecessarily. It means distinguishing what the organisation needs to know from details that merely identify who spoke. It means explaining, where possible, what will happen with the information before acting on it.

Handled poorly, one disclosure can close a channel that took months to establish. Handled well, it can demonstrate that difficult information can enter the organisation without the person supplying it becoming the problem.

## Trust is not neutrality

Standing between management and workers does not mean remaining neutral about everything.

A safety professional should not become a private repository for problems that never lead anywhere. Listening sympathetically while leaving a serious risk untouched is not trustworthiness—it is passivity.

Nor should trust with workers require automatic opposition to management. A credible safety professional must sometimes tell workers that the control is necessary, that the evidence does not support their account or that their preferred solution is not reasonably practicable.

The role is not to take whichever side is speaking, but to treat the information seriously, test it properly and advise the organisation honestly—even when the conclusion is inconvenient to the person who raised the issue.

That is another reason not to make promises too early. If workers understand that you will listen without prejudging the outcome, disagreement does not have to feel like betrayal.

## Workers remember what follows disclosure

Workers form their view of a safety professional cumulatively. Each disclosure becomes another test of whether uncomfortable information can be handled without reflexive judgement, unnecessary exposure or an immediate search for somebody to blame.

Over time, they learn whether you can take the good with the bad, filter the noise and pursue useful changes that do not begin with penalising the individual. They also learn whether you will act when the information genuinely requires action, even if the eventual response is not the one they wanted.

That reputation is not established by assuring people that your door is always open. It is established by what happens after somebody walks through it.

A useful safety conversation therefore begins before the first question is asked. It begins with the workers’ accumulated experience of the person asking it.

They need to know that uncomfortable information will be considered rather than punished reflexively. They need to know that listening does not depend on perfect language, a calm temperament or an account that flatters management. They need to know that the person receiving the information can tolerate uncertainty long enough to understand it.

The quality of a safety conversation is measured less by how confidently the question was delivered or how quickly the room agreed than by whether workers would still tell you the truth after everyone else has left.
